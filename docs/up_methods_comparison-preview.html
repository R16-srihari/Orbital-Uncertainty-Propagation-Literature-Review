Monte Carlo (MC) Provides the most direct and general representation of the propagated PDF; minimal assumptions on the dynamics or distribution Extremely computationally expensive; slow statistical convergence; inefficient for rare-event probability estimation Statistical error decreases only as (\(1/\sqrt{N}\)); high-fidelity dynamics must be integrated for every sample Excellent as a reference or validation method, but often unsuitable for real-time or large-scale operational conjunction assessment — — —
Linearised Covariance (LinCov) Very high computational efficiency; simple and suitable for onboard implementation Assumes local linearity and usually Gaussian uncertainty; cannot capture higher-order distributional deformation The linearized state transition model becomes inaccurate as uncertainty grows or the dynamics become strongly nonlinear Effective for short propagation times and small uncertainties; unreliable for long-term propagation, highly elliptical orbits, and non-Gaussian PDFs — — —
Unscented Transform (UT) Captures nonlinear effects more accurately than first-order linearization with relatively few propagated points Primarily represents only low-order statistics; generally assumes that mean and covariance sufficiently describe the distribution A finite sigma-point set cannot reproduce strongly skewed, multimodal, or highly deformed PDFs Efficient for moderate nonlinearities and Gaussian-like uncertainties, but inadequate as a standalone method for strongly non-Gaussian long-term propagation — — —
Polynomial Chaos Expansion (PCE) Can represent nonlinear input-output mappings and highly non-Gaussian propagated distributions with high accuracy Suffers from the curse of dimensionality; computational cost rises rapidly with polynomial order and state dimension Number of polynomial basis terms increases combinatorially; regression or projection may become ill-conditioned or fail to converge Powerful for low- to moderate-dimensional problems, but difficult to scale to high-dimensional orbital uncertainty spaces — — —
PCE — discontinuous/non-smooth dynamics Flexible surrogate representation Poor performance near discontinuities or non-smooth force-model transitions Global polynomial approximation can exhibit Gibbs-type oscillations and local approximation errors Requires domain decomposition, multi-element PCE, or other adaptive techniques when discontinuities are present — — —
PCE — long-duration propagation Maintains high accuracy when a suitable expansion remains valid A single global polynomial may become inaccurate over large propagated uncertainty domains Increasing nonlinearity causes the surrogate to lose accuracy and may require domain splitting Long-term orbital propagation generally requires adaptive or multi-element formulations — — —
State Transition Tensors (STT) Extends the STM concept to higher-order nonlinear mappings; can directly propagate higher-order statistics High computational and derivational complexity; requires high-order derivatives or variational equations Number of tensor terms grows rapidly with state dimension and expansion order Attractive for high-accuracy local nonlinear propagation, but difficult to implement for high-fidelity, high-dimensional dynamics — — —
STT — local validity Provides a high-order local Taylor representation Limited radius of convergence The Taylor expansion may become inaccurate or diverge when the uncertainty region extends far from the nominal trajectory Large initial uncertainties and strongly nonlinear orbital regimes may require domain splitting or multiple local expansions — — —
STT — discontinuities and maneuvers Effective for smooth nonlinear dynamics Difficult to apply directly to discontinuous dynamics, impulsive events, and piecewise force models Derivative-based expansion is not naturally compatible with state jumps or non-differentiable transitions Maneuver segmentation and special transition mappings are required — — —
Differential Algebra (DA) Automates high-order Taylor expansion and avoids manual derivation of high-order derivatives Expansion order, memory, and number of polynomial terms can become large High-dimensional high-order polynomial representations create computational and memory overhead More practical than manually derived STTs, but still subject to local expansion and truncation limitations — — —
DA with automatic domain splitting Extends the region over which polynomial representations remain accurate Requires additional splitting logic and repeated local representations Excessive splitting can create a large number of domains or polynomial maps Accuracy can be maintained at the cost of increasing computational and memory requirements — — —
DA — finite PDF representation Can calculate high-order statistical information The propagated uncertainty is still represented through a finite-dimensional polynomial approximation The polynomial representation is not an exact arbitrary PDF May not accurately reproduce complex tails or highly multimodal distributions without additional decomposition — — —
Stochastic Collocation (SC) Multi-Fidelity Reduces the number of expensive high-fidelity simulations by exploiting a cheap low-fidelity model Accuracy depends strongly on the relationship between low- and high-fidelity dynamics Poor correlation between fidelity levels produces inaccurate corrections or surrogates Highly effective when the low-fidelity model captures the dominant dynamics; unreliable when model discrepancy is large — — —
SC Multi-Fidelity — rank limitation Uses a small number of important high-fidelity samples The number of independent correction directions can be limited by the rank/dimensionality of the approximation A small number of basis directions may be insufficient to represent complex propagated PDFs Efficiency is high, but representational capacity can be limited for highly complex distributions — — —
SC Multi-Fidelity — tolerance sensitivity Allows control of computational effort and approximation accuracy Results depend on low-fidelity and surrogate tolerances Poor tolerance selection can either waste computation or produce inaccurate uncertainty distributions Requires careful parameter tuning and problem-specific calibration — — —
DA / LOADS-type adaptive MF methods Detect nonlinear regions and refine the representation adaptively Adaptive splitting can generate many local models Strong nonlinearities lead to domain proliferation and increased computational cost Improves robustness but may reduce the computational advantage of the original surrogate — — —
PLASMA / stochastic moment-based MF approaches Efficiently propagates statistical information using low-order moments Accuracy depends on the adequacy of the selected moment order and approximation Higher-order nonlinear effects may not be represented by a limited moment set Suitable when the distribution remains sufficiently regular; less reliable for strongly non-Gaussian PDFs — — —
AEGIS Adaptively splits Gaussian components based on detected nonlinear deformation Computational cost can grow rapidly as the number of mixture components increases Repeated splitting causes component proliferation; delayed splitting can lose information about accumulated nonlinear deformation Can represent nonlinear deformation, but may become impractical for long propagation times and strongly nonlinear regimes — — —
AEGIS — correlation buildup Adaptive entropy-based refinement Component correlations can become increasingly complex Components may spread across irrelevant directions or deviate from the true distribution Requires improved splitting strategies such as multidirectional or hybrid adaptive methods
Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) Can represent non-Gaussian and multimodal PDFs using a mixture of Gaussian components Accuracy depends on the number, placement, covariance, and weights of components Insufficient components fail to capture tails, skewness, multimodality, or sharp deformation Flexible but computationally sensitive; component management becomes a central problem — — —
GMM–UT Combines distribution decomposition with efficient sigma-point propagation Accuracy depends on both the initial mixture representation and the UT approximation Each Gaussian component is still propagated using a low-order statistical approximation More capable than a single UT, but may still poorly represent extreme tails of highly non-Gaussian distributions — — —
GMM–STT Combines local Gaussian decomposition with high-order nonlinear mapping High-order tensor calculations become expensive for many components Computational complexity increases with mixture size, state dimension, and tensor order Accurate for suitable local regions, but scalability is a major limitation — — —
GMM–DA Uses automatic polynomial mappings for each mixture component Polynomial order and mixture size can produce substantial memory and processing requirements Truncation errors increase away from each expansion point; tails may be poorly represented More flexible than manually derived STTs but still requires careful order and component management — — —
GMM–PCE Reduces the effective nonlinearity experienced by each polynomial expansion Still inherits the dimensionality problem of PCE and the approximation error of the initial GMM Accuracy is bounded by the quality of the initial mixture decomposition Effective hybrid approach, but not a complete solution to high-dimensionality — — —
GMM–MF Uses low-fidelity dynamics to efficiently propagate mixture components or important samples Accuracy depends on low-/high-fidelity correlation and the number of important samples Insufficient correction samples cannot capture all relevant uncertainty directions Offers excellent computational efficiency, but requires careful selection of fidelity models and correction basis — — —
GMM–Moment Method Efficiently propagates low-order statistics of individual Gaussian components Primarily limited to the information contained in the propagated moments Linearization or truncated Taylor approximations become inaccurate in regions of high curvature Computationally attractive, but accuracy decreases as component spread and nonlinear deformation increase — — —
Moment-Based Propagation in General Much cheaper than full PDF propagation Higher-order and tail information may be discarded Different PDFs can share the same mean and covariance but have very different shapes Mean and covariance alone may be insufficient for conjunction assessment and rare-event collision probabilities — — —

Cross-Cutting Limitations

General Challenge Methods Most Affected Consequence
Strong nonlinear dynamics LinCov, UT, moment methods, low-order STT/DA Gaussian distributions deform into skewed, curved, or multimodal PDFs
Long propagation duration LinCov, UT, local STT, single-domain DA, single-domain PCE Local approximations progressively lose validity
High state dimensionality PCE, STT, DA, GMM, FPE-based methods Computational and memory requirements grow rapidly
Non-Gaussian uncertainty LinCov, UT, low-order moment methods Important skewness, kurtosis, tails, and multimodality may be lost
Rare-event collision probability MC, moment-based methods, Gaussian approximations Accurate estimation of very small P_C values becomes difficult
Model uncertainty All methods Propagating state uncertainty accurately does not compensate for an inaccurate force model
Initial uncertainty characterization All methods A highly accurate propagator cannot produce a reliable final PDF from an incorrect initial PDF
Discontinuous dynamics and maneuvers STT, DA, PCE, derivative-based methods Taylor and polynomial representations may become invalid across discontinuities
Low-/high-fidelity model mismatch Multi-fidelity methods The computational advantage can be lost or the propagated PDF can become biased
Component proliferation GMM and AEGIS-based methods Adaptive refinement may eventually become computationally comparable to direct sampling
Operational integration Most advanced nonlinear methods Accurate PDFs are not automatically converted into reliable operational decisions such as collision alerts

Overall Assessment

The central limitation of orbital uncertainty propagation is the accuracy–computational-cost trade-off. No single method simultaneously provides:

  1. the generality of Monte Carlo,
  2. the computational efficiency of linear covariance propagation,
  3. the nonlinear accuracy of high-order Taylor or polynomial methods, and
  4. the full-PDF representation capability of adaptive GMM or density-based methods.
